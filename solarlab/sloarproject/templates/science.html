The Science

One idea explains everything in the simulator: a solar panel makes the most power when sunlight hits it straight on.

Lambert's cosine law

Imagine holding a sheet of paper in a torch beam. Face it straight at the torch and it catches the whole beam. Tilt it and it catches less, because the same light is spread over a slanted surface. Solar panels work the same way.

P = 100 W × weather × intensity × (1 − shade) × cos θ

θ (theta)
The angle between the sunlight and the line pointing straight out of the panel. 0° means the panel faces the sun.
cos (cosine)
A number between 1 and 0 that says how much light is caught. It is 1 when θ = 0° and 0 when θ = 90°.
Weather factor
The share of light that gets through clouds: 1.0 sunny, 0.6 partly cloudy, 0.25 cloudy.
Shade
The fraction of the panel covered by a shadow, from 0 (none) to 1 (all of it).

Worked example

A sunny day, full-strength sun, no shade, a 100 W panel:

θcos θPower
0°1.00100 W
30°0.8786.6 W
45°0.7170.7 W
60°0.5050 W
90°0.000 W

Small angles cost very little. A panel 30° off still gives about 87% of full power. The loss speeds up after that.

Finding the best tilt

  1. Measure how high the sun is above the horizon.
  2. The best tilt is 90° minus that height.
  3. That makes θ = 0°, so cos θ = 1. Example: sun at 30°, best tilt 60°.

What this model leaves out

The weather values are assumed for the demo, not measured. A real panel also loses power when it gets hot, and reflects more light at very steep angles. Treat the numbers as a way to understand the idea, not as a forecast.
