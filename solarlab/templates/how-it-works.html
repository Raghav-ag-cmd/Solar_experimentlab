How the simulator works

What every function does, and how the formula grew from the original cosine law to the final one as more factors were added.

The big picture

Everything starts when you move a control. The simulator then repeats the same few steps, so the picture, the numbers and the graphs always agree.

  1. You move a slider (tilt, sun height, intensity, shade) or change the weather.
  2. update() reads every control and works out θ with calcTheta().
  3. calcPower() puts θ, weather, intensity and shade into the formula to get watts.
  4. The scene, the numbers, the comparison text, the graphs and the live line are redrawn from that one result.

Where θ comes from

The panel faces upward by 90° − tilt. The sun is sunHeight above the horizon. θ is the gap between those two directions, so θ = |(90° − tilt) − sunHeight|. When the gap is 0°, the panel looks straight at the sun and gets all the light. That is why the best tilt is 90° − sun height.

What each function does

Maths and charts (core.js)

FunctionWhat it does, in plain words
degToRad(deg)Turns degrees into radians. JavaScript's Math.cos only understands radians, so every angle goes through this first.
calcTheta(tilt, sunHeight)Finds θ, the angle between the sunlight and the line pointing straight out of the panel: |(90 − tilt) − sunHeight|.
calcPower(...)The formula itself. Multiplies the 100 W rating by weather, intensity, the unshaded share and cos(θ). Returns 0 if θ is 90° or more.
readColors()Reads the colours from the CSS so the drawings match the light or dark theme and the colour style.
barChart(...)Draws a bar chart on a canvas. A bar's height is its value ÷ the maximum × the height of the chart.
groupedBarChart(...)The same, with two bars per label (Panel A and Panel B side by side).
lineChart(...)Draws the live graph and the best-angle curve by joining the points with a line.

How the formula grew

The model started with one idea and gained a factor each time a new real-world effect was added. The example below uses the same conditions each time: sun at 30°, panel flat (tilt 0°, so θ = 60° and cos θ = 0.5), cloudy sky (w = 0.25), 80% sunlight (I = 0.8) and 40% shade (s = 0.4). Watch the result shrink as each factor joins.

Stage 1

The cosine law

P = Pmax × cos(θ)

Only the angle matters. A perfectly clear sky, full-strength sun, nothing in the way.

Example result: 50.0 W

Stage 2

Add sunlight intensity

P = Pmax × I × cos(θ)

A slider for how strong the sun is, from 0 to 1. At 80%, I = 0.8.

Example result: 40.0 W

Stage 3

Add weather

P = Pmax × w × I × cos(θ)

Clouds let only part of the light through. Cloudy is w = 0.25.

Example result: 10.0 W

Stage 4

Add shade (the final formula)

P = Pmax × w × I × (1 − s) × cos(θ)

A tree or building covers part of the panel. With 40% covered, s = 0.4, so 60% is left.

Example result: 6.0 W

Why the factors are multiplied

Each factor is the fraction of light that survives one effect. If clouds let through 25% and then the shade lets through 60% of that, you keep 0.25 × 0.6 = 15%. Fractions of fractions multiply, and the order does not matter. Adding them would give nonsense, such as a dark, cloudy, shaded panel making more power than a clear one.

Two rules in the code

  • No negative power. If θ is 90° or more, the light misses the front of the panel and calcPower() returns 0.
  • Shade is off until you ask. With shade off, s = 0, so (1 − s) = 1 and the formula is the same as Stage 3. This is why the old and new answers match when shade is not used.

Assumptions: the weather values (1.0, 0.6, 0.25) are assumed for the demonstration, and intensity and shade are treated as straight percentages. Real panels lose more power when shade falls across their cells, and they also lose power when hot or dusty. Use the simulator to compare conditions and see trends, not to predict real output.
